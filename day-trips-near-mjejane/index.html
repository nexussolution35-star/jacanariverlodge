Graskop Gorge Lift and Day Trips near Mjejane

The Graskop Gorge, God’s Window, Bourke’s Luck Potholes, the Three Rondavels and the Blyde River Canyon on the Panorama Route, plus Chimp Eden and the Jabulani Elephant Sanctuary.

Jacana River Lodge in the Mjejane Private Game Reserve is a good base for a day on the escarpment. The journey from Mjejane to the first stop on the Panorama Route, Mac Mac Falls, is approximately 2-3 hours, and the lodge can arrange a full- or half-day Panorama tour. Here is what to expect at the highlights.

Graskop Gorge Lift

Just south of Graskop along the R533 you will find the Graskop Gorge Lift Company. It is centrally located on the Panorama Route and makes an ideal stopover, whether for the view, the forest, the food or the adventure activities, such as guided tours and hikes in the surrounding area. When we wrote about it, it was open daily from 8.30 am to 5 pm.

Set in a pristine natural environment, the experience starts with an enchanting 51-metre elevator ride all the way down a cliff into the cool gorge below. Otis manufactured the lift. It carries 26 passengers, is partially glass-fronted for views across the escarpment and takes about a minute to complete a trip down. The lift is wheelchair-accessible, as is part of the trail.

The Graskop Gorge forest trail

Down in the Graskop Gorge, an extensive network of sturdy elevated walkways and suspension bridges leads from the spectacular 80-metre Panorama Falls along the Motitsi River on a 600-metre forest trail. The trail is geared towards eco-education, with six interpretation exhibits on the forest and its biodiversity, water conservation, birds and insects, giant replicas of indigenous mushrooms and a stained-glass window.

You can complete the trail in about an hour. It is not too strenuous, but it has several stairs and steep inclines; the first part is accessible to all, including wheelchairs. The climate is generally wet, so sections can be slippery. The decks have anti-slip treads, but take care.

The Big Swing, zipline and suspension bridge

From the top you can watch the brave take on The Big Swing, described as the world’s highest cable gorge swing. The jump can be done solo or in tandem, with participants strapped upright into safety harnesses for a 70-metre, 140 km/hour freefall. For the slightly less adventurous there is a 130 m high-wire zipline across the gorge, or a 52-metre suspension bridge spanning it.

The Lift Café and activity centre

The activity centre at the top has enough to keep visitors busy for a few hours, with a coffee shop and restaurant, The Lift Café, an art gallery, a curio shop and informal arts and crafts stalls.

What to take to the Graskop Gorge

  • Comfortable rubber-soled walking shoes
  • A raincoat or umbrella in the rain or if the waterfall is full
  • Camera and binoculars
  • Water

For up-to-date rates and information, visit the Graskop Gorge Lift Company website, or read our full post, Graskop Gorge: an uplifting experience.

The Panorama Route: Blyde River Canyon, God’s Window and More

One of the country’s most scenic self-drives, the Panorama Route explores the Mpumalanga highlands, the north-eastern section of the Great Escarpment of the Drakensberg, where the plateau comes to an abrupt halt and falls steeply away into the Lowveld. The most popular stretch is the R532 from Sabie, past a group of spectacular waterfalls (the Sabie, Bridal Veil and Mac Mac falls) to God’s Window, and from there to the Blyde River Canyon, Bourke’s Luck Potholes and the Three Rondavels. Read more in our post on Panorama Route Mpumalanga day tours.

Blyde River Canyon

The Blyde River Canyon is the third largest canyon in the world, carved over centuries by the Blyde River. The iconic view over the canyon, with the Blyde River cutting through the valley, is less than 5 km north of Bourke’s Luck Potholes. The cost of a visit depends on what you choose to do, from guided tours and boat trips to self-guided exploration, so check with local tour operators or visitor centres for current prices.

God’s Window

God’s Window is a renowned viewpoint with a breathtaking vista over the Lowveld. Visit early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when the light is often ideal and there are fewer crowds. In summer, mist and rain can hamper sightseeing, especially at God’s Window, so keep this in mind when planning. Entrance fees may vary; check with the local authorities or visitor information centres.

Bourke’s Luck Potholes

Bourke’s Luck Potholes is a geological wonderland of swirling rock formations, potholes and waterfalls, where swirling whirlpools have carved the potholes over centuries. Entrance fees can vary, so check with the relevant authorities or visitor information centres before you go.

The Three Rondavels

The Three Rondavels are a set of distinctive rock formations named after their resemblance to traditional round African houses called “rondavels”. They offer stunning views of the Blyde River Canyon and the surrounding landscapes. There is no specific entrance fee: the formations can be admired from designated viewpoints along the Panorama Route.

Good to know before you go

Drive carefully: stick well below the speed limit in heavily potholed areas, especially at night and after heavy rains, and switch on your hazard lights in thick mist. Pack snacks: once you hit the road it can be hard to find things to snack on, so take a picnic lunch with plenty of water and cool drinks, especially in summer. Bring some cash for entrance fees, and keep a weatherproof jacket in the car for sudden changes in temperature. Take a hat and sunscreen, and stick to the demarcated paths.

Chimp Eden (Jane Goodall Institute)

A short 15km outside Nelspruit, visitors to Mpumalanga can meet one of the world’s most endangered animals. The chimpanzees are not native to South Africa: displaced from their natural habitat and rescued elsewhere on the continent, they have been offered a safe haven at JGI Chimpanzee Eden.

The sanctuary is set in the 1000 hectare Umhloti Nature Reserve. Established in 2006, it is the only one of its kind in the country, committed to the rescue and care of chimpanzees in need of refuge and serving as an educational and eco-tourism facility. The chimpanzees spend their days in semi-wild enclosures; there are three chimp groups and enclosures, with viewpoints over the forest and foraging areas.

Guided tours take place three times a day (weather permitting), twice around feeding time, and last about 1,5 hours. Light meals and refreshments are available from the restaurant before or after the tour. The Institute is a non-profit organisation, and all funds received go back into JGI Chimp Eden and its Roots and Shoots outreach programmes.

Visits are arranged with the sanctuary itself: see the Chimp Eden website for more information, or read our post on Chimp Eden, a safe haven for the hunted ones.

Jabulani Elephant Sanctuary

Jabulani Elephant Sanctuary, in the Kapama Private Game Reserve, offers visitors the chance to meet an extraordinary family: the Jabulani elephants. The story started with an injured four-month-old elephant calf, found stuck and abandoned in a mud pool and brought to the Hoedspruit Endangered Species Centre. In 2002 the centre became involved in the rescue of 12 elephants from a commercial farm in Zimbabwe, who immediately accepted Jabulani as one of their own. Because of their dependency on humans, releasing them into the wild was never an option, and Camp Jabulani ended its elephant-back safaris in 2006 in favour of a more observational, ethical approach.

  • Interaction: a 90-minute sunrise orientation to meet some of the more outgoing members of the herd and hear their keepers’ stories.
  • Sunset and stable walk: watch the elephants’ final stop at a watering hole, then follow them back to the stables.
  • Elephant swim: in summer, quietly observe the herd’s midday swim from an open safari vehicle.
  • Tracking the elephants: follow the herd through the bush with a ranger, trails guide and senior elephant carer.

Book directly with Jabulani: see the Jabulani website for more information, or read our post on encountering elephants the ethical way at Jabulani.
